🦺 Scene safety & calling 911

  1. Stop and look before rushing in: traffic, fire, smoke, downed wires, water, violence, unstable structures. If unsafe, stay back and call 911.
  2. Call 911 (or point at one person: "You — call 911 and come back"). Give the address/location first, then what happened and how many are hurt. Don't hang up until told to.
  3. Send someone for an AED and first-aid kit.
  4. Use gloves or a barrier if available. Get consent from conscious adults; consent is implied for unresponsive people.
  5. Don't move an injured person unless they're in danger (possible spine injury).

🚨 Emergency priorities (in order)

  1. 🦺 Scene safe?
  2. 📞 Call 911 and get an AED.
  3. 🩸 Life-threatening bleeding — control it immediately.
  4. 🫁 Responsive? Breathing normally? If unresponsive and not breathing normally (or only gasping): start CPR.
  5. 🛌 Shock: keep them lying down, warm, calm; nothing to eat or drink.
  6. 🩹 Then treat other injuries (burns, fractures, wounds), and keep monitoring breathing until help arrives.

🩸 Bleeding control (Stop the Bleed)

  1. Call 911. Find the source of bleeding — expose the wound.
  2. Direct pressure: press hard with both hands on the wound using gauze or a clean cloth. Don't lift to check; add more layers on top if it soaks through.
  3. Wound packing (deep wounds of arm, leg, neck, armpit or groin): stuff gauze/clean cloth tightly into the wound until full, then keep pushing hard for at least 3 minutes.
  4. Tourniquet (life-threatening arm/leg bleeding that pressure doesn't stop): place it 2–3 inches above the wound (not on a joint), pull tight, twist the rod until bleeding stops, lock it, and note the time. It will hurt — that's expected. Never loosen it; leave that to medical staff.
  5. Don't use tourniquets on the neck, chest or abdomen — use pressure/packing.
🎓 Free Stop the Bleed classes teach this in about an hour. Commercial tourniquets (e.g. CAT, SOF-T) work much better than improvised ones.

🧼 Wound care & infection signs

  1. Wash your hands; wear gloves if you have them.
  2. Stop minor bleeding with pressure.
  3. Rinse the wound under clean running water; clean around it with soap. Remove surface dirt; don't dig for deep objects.
  4. Cover with a clean bandage; change it daily or when wet/dirty.

Seek care for deep or gaping cuts, bites (animal or human), puncture wounds, wounds with embedded objects, or if tetanus vaccination isn't up to date (generally every 10 years; 5 for dirty wounds).

🚩 Infection signs: increasing redness or warmth, swelling, pus, worsening pain, red streaks spreading from the wound, or fever → see a doctor promptly. Wounds exposed to flood or warm sea water (e.g. Florida, the Gulf) can develop serious Vibrio infections — seek care quickly.

🔥 Burns

  1. Stop the burning: remove from the source; remove jewelry and tight items near the burn (not clothing stuck to skin).
  2. Cool with cool running water for about 20 minutes (within the first few hours).
  3. No ice, butter, oils or toothpaste. Don't pop blisters.
  4. Cover loosely with a clean, non-stick dressing or plastic wrap.
📞 Call 911 / seek care for burns that are large (bigger than the person's palm), deep (white, leathery or charred), on the face, hands, feet, genitals or major joints, chemical or electrical burns, burns with breathing trouble or smoke inhalation, and burns in young children or older adults.

🦴 Fractures & sprains

  1. Call 911 for open fractures (bone through skin), suspected head/neck/back, hip or thigh injuries, or if the limb is cold, blue or numb.
  2. Don't straighten a deformed limb. Support it in the position found.
  3. Splint only if you must move the person and it doesn't cause more pain: pad a rigid object (board, rolled magazine) and tie it to immobilize the joints above and below the injury. Check fingers/toes for warmth and color after.
  4. Sprains — RICE: Rest, Ice (wrapped in cloth, 20 minutes at a time), Compression (elastic bandage, not too tight), Elevation above heart level.

❤️ CPR basics & AED

Adults — Hands-Only CPR

  1. Unresponsive and not breathing normally (or only gasping)? Call 911, get an AED.
  2. Place the heel of one hand in the center of the chest, other hand on top.
  3. Push hard and fast: at least 2 inches deep, 100–120 compressions per minute (the beat of "Stayin' Alive"). Let the chest fully recoil.
  4. Don't stop until an AED is ready, EMS takes over, or the person starts breathing.

⚡ AED

  1. Turn it on and follow the voice prompts.
  2. Bare the chest (wipe it dry), attach pads as pictured.
  3. Everyone stand clear while it analyzes and shocks; resume CPR right away when told.
🎓 Children, infants and drowning victims benefit from rescue breaths with compressions — take an AHA or Red Cross CPR course to learn them properly.

😮‍💨 Choking

If the person can cough forcefully or speak, encourage coughing. If they can't breathe, cough or speak:

Adults & children over 1

  1. Have someone call 911.
  2. Red Cross method: 5 back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand, then 5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich: fist just above the navel, quick inward-upward thrusts). AHA teaches abdominal thrusts alone — either is acceptable.
  3. Repeat until the object comes out or they become unresponsive. For pregnant or very large people, use chest thrusts.
  4. If they become unresponsive: lower them to the ground, start CPR, and look in the mouth for the object before breaths.

👶 Infants (under 1)

  1. Hold face-down along your forearm, head lower than chest, supporting the jaw.
  2. Give 5 back blows between the shoulder blades.
  3. Turn face-up and give 5 chest thrusts with 2 fingers just below the nipple line.
  4. Repeat. Never do blind finger sweeps. If unresponsive, call 911 and start infant CPR.

🐝 Allergic reactions & anaphylaxis

  1. Mild (itchy rash, hives only): remove the trigger; an antihistamine may help — follow the label. Watch closely for worsening.
  2. Anaphylaxis signs: trouble breathing, wheezing, throat/tongue swelling, hoarse voice, widespread hives with dizziness, vomiting, fainting.
  3. Use the person's epinephrine auto-injector (EpiPen, Auvi-Q, etc.) right away — outer thigh, through clothing if needed — following its instructions.
  4. Call 911 even if symptoms improve; a second reaction can occur. A second dose may be needed per the device instructions if no improvement in 5–15 minutes.
  5. Have them sit up if breathing is hard, or lie down with legs raised if faint.

🌡 Heat illness

🌴 In hot, humid climates the heat index can be dangerous much of the year, especially during power outages with no A/C. Check on older adults, kids, outdoor workers and people with chronic illness. Never leave kids or pets in cars.

Heat exhaustion

Heavy sweating, cold clammy skin, fast weak pulse, nausea, cramps, dizziness, headache.

  1. Move to shade or A/C; loosen clothing.
  2. Cool wet cloths, fan; sip water or electrolyte drinks.
  3. Get medical help if vomiting, symptoms worsen or last over 1 hour.

Heat stroke — emergency

High body temperature (103°F+), hot red dry or damp skin, confusion, slurred speech, seizures, collapse.

  1. Call 911.
  2. Cool immediately: cold water immersion if possible, or soak with water and fan; ice packs to neck, armpits and groin.
  3. Don't give fluids to someone who is confused or unresponsive.

💊 Medication storage & expiration

  1. Store medicines in a cool, dry place away from sunlight — not the bathroom, car or a hot garage. Keep them in original labeled containers, out of reach of children.
  2. Expiration: FDA advises not using expired medicines; potency can drop and some (e.g. liquid antibiotics, insulin, epinephrine, nitroglycerin) can become ineffective. In emergencies, FDA sometimes issues specific guidance — check fda.gov or ask a pharmacist.
  3. Keep a written list of medicines, doses as prescribed, allergies and doctors; refill early before hurricane season and storms (some places, e.g. Florida, allow early emergency refills when a state of emergency is declared).
  4. No dosing advice here — take medicines only as prescribed or as the label directs; ask a pharmacist.

🧊 Insulin when the power is out (FDA/CDC)

  1. Unopened insulin is best refrigerated (36–46°F), but in an emergency insulin can be kept at room temperature (59–86°F) for up to about 28 days (check your product's label — times vary).
  2. Keep it out of direct heat and sunlight and never freeze it. Use a cooler with ice packs wrapped in cloth (don't let insulin touch ice) or an insulin cooling case.
  3. Check for clumps, frosting or color change before use; if in doubt, replace it as soon as possible. Contact your doctor or pharmacist about replacement.
