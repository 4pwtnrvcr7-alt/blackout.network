⚠️ Educational guide only. Beginner-level, conservative summary of widely published guidance (Ready.gov/FEMA, CDC, EPA, American Red Cross, US Army survival manual principles). It is not professional, medical or legal advice. In an emergency call 911. Follow local officials and evacuation orders first.

⏱ First 72 hours — do these in order

1. 🦺 Safety first

  1. Get away from immediate danger: fire, flood water, downed power lines, gas smell, damaged buildings.
  2. Check people for injuries; call 911 if lines work. Treat life threats first (see 🩹 First aid).
  3. Smell gas? Leave, don't flip switches, and call the gas company or 911 from outside.
  4. Never use a generator, grill or camp stove indoors, in a garage or near windows (carbon monoxide kills).
  5. Stay out of flood water: it can hide holes and live wires and is often contaminated.

2. 3️⃣ The Rule of 3s (rough guide to priorities)

3 minuteswithout air, or with severe bleeding
3 hourswithout shelter in harsh heat or cold
3 dayswithout water
3 weekswithout food

These are rough rules of thumb, not exact limits. In hot, humid heat you can get into trouble without water much faster.

3. 💧 Water

  1. Store or secure at least 1 gallon per person per day (more in heat, for kids, nursing mothers and the sick). Plan at least 3 days; Ready.gov suggests 2 weeks at home if you can.
  2. Fill clean containers and bathtubs before a storm hits. Don't ration water you have — drink what you need today.
  3. If the water system is under a boil-water notice, treat all drinking/cooking water (see 💧 Water).

4. ⛺ Shelter & warmth (or cooling)

  1. Stay in a safe, structurally sound building if you can; go to a public shelter if told to evacuate.
  2. In hot, humid heat: shade, airflow, wet cloth on the skin, rest in the hottest hours. In cold snaps: layers, blankets, one warm room.

5. 📻 Communication & information

  1. Battery or hand-crank NOAA Weather Radio and AM/FM radio for official info.
  2. Text instead of calling (texts get through congested networks more often). Save phone battery: low-power mode, dim screen.
  3. Agree on an out-of-area contact and a family meeting place.

6. 🍲 Food (last — you have time)

  1. Eat perishable food from the fridge first, then freezer, then shelf-stable food.
  2. A closed fridge keeps food cold about 4 hours; a full closed freezer about 48 hours (24 if half full). When in doubt, throw it out.

7. 🎒 Go-bag check

  • Water (1 gal/person/day) and water treatment (filter, tablets, unscented bleach + dropper)
  • 3 days of non-perishable food + manual can opener
  • Flashlight/headlamp, spare batteries, NOAA weather radio
  • First-aid kit, prescription meds (7-day supply if possible), glasses
  • Copies of IDs, insurance, medical info in a waterproof bag; some cash
  • Phone charger + power bank; paper map of the area
  • Whistle, dust masks, work gloves, duct tape, multi-tool
  • Rain gear, change of clothes, sturdy shoes, bug spray, sunscreen
  • Hygiene items, wipes, garbage bags; pet food and carrier
  • Lighter/matches in a waterproof container, emergency blanket

💧 Water

🔎 Finding water

  1. Inside your home first: stored water, water heater tank (turn off power/gas first, drain from the bottom valve), ice cubes, toilet tank (not bowl; not if it has chemical cleaners).
  2. Outside: rain water collected in clean containers, springs, streams, lakes. Moving, clear water is better than stagnant, but all outdoor water must be treated.
  3. Avoid water with a chemical smell, oily sheen, dead animals nearby, or that may be flood water or near farm/industrial runoff — boiling doesn't remove chemicals.

🧹 Filter

  1. Let cloudy water settle, then pour it through a clean cloth, paper towel or coffee filter.
  2. A commercial backpacking filter removes bacteria and protozoa; most don't remove viruses — boil or disinfect too if unsure.

♨️ Boil (best method)

  1. Bring clear water to a rolling boil for 1 minute.
  2. Above 6,500 ft elevation, boil for 3 minutes (CDC).
  3. Let it cool; store covered in clean containers. Pour between containers to improve taste.

🧴 Disinfect with household bleach (if you can't boil)

  1. Use only regular, unscented chlorine bleach (no color-safe, scented or added cleaners).
  2. For 6% bleach: 8 drops (about 1/8 teaspoon) per gallon of clear water. (8.25% bleach: 6 drops per gallon.) Double the dose if water is cloudy or very cold after filtering.
  3. Stir, wait at least 30 minutes. It should have a slight chlorine smell; if not, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes.
💊 Tablets: follow the package directions exactly (contact times vary, often 30 minutes to 4 hours). Chlorine dioxide tablets work against more organisms than chlorine alone.

⛺ Shelter

📍 Site selection

  1. High, well-drained ground — not in a dry wash, ditch or flood zone.
  2. Look up: avoid dead trees and hanging branches ("widowmakers").
  3. Away from ant mounds, wasp nests, animal trails and standing water (mosquitoes).
  4. Near (but not right next to) water, with shelter from wind and storms.

🛏 Insulate from the ground

The ground pulls heat out of you fast, even in mild weather. Sleep on a pad, cardboard, or a thick (15+ cm / 6+ in) bed of dry leaves, pine straw or palm fronds.

🍂 Debris hut basics

  1. Prop a long ridge pole on a stump or forked stick, about hip height, angled to the ground.
  2. Lean sticks along both sides like ribs.
  3. Pile dry leaves/debris very thick (an arm's length) over the frame; stuff the inside with dry leaves.
  4. Keep it small — just big enough for your body — so it holds warmth.

🟦 Tarp basics

  1. Tie a ridge line between two trees; drape the tarp over it (A-frame) or tie one edge low to the ground (lean-to) facing away from wind and rain.
  2. Stake corners tight; keep a slope so rain runs off.
🌴 Hot-humid climate notes (e.g. Florida, the Gulf Coast, the tropics): shade and airflow matter more than warmth most of the year. Pitch the tarp high with open sides for ventilation, keep off the wet ground, use a bug net, long sleeves and repellent (mosquitoes carry disease), check for ticks and fire ants, and watch for snakes when moving debris. Lightning is common in summer afternoons — avoid lone tall trees and open high ground.

🔥 Fire

🔺 The fire triangle

Fire needs heat + fuel + oxygen. Remove any one and it goes out.

🪵 Build it in stages

  1. Tinder (catches a spark): dry grass, cattail fluff, birch bark, dry pine needles, cotton balls with petroleum jelly, dryer lint.
  2. Kindling (pencil-thick dry sticks): add gently once tinder flames.
  3. Fuel (wrist-thick and bigger): add gradually; don't smother it.
  4. Gather all three before you strike. Dead branches still on trees are usually drier than ground wood.

🔦 Ignition

  • Lighter — easiest; carry two.
  • Matches — keep waterproof/stormproof ones in a sealed container.
  • Ferro rod — scrape the striker firmly down the rod into fluffy tinder; works wet. Practice before you need it.

🧯 Safety

  1. Clear a 3 m (10 ft) circle down to bare soil; use an existing fire ring if there is one. Check for local burn bans (your forestry or fire agency).
  2. Never leave it unattended; keep water/sand nearby. Drown, stir and feel for heat before leaving.
  3. ☠️ Never run generators, grills, charcoal or camp stoves indoors, in garages or tents. Carbon monoxide is invisible and odorless. Keep generators at least 20 ft from doors and windows and use battery CO alarms.

🍲 Food

🥫 Stored food first

  1. Eat perishables first, then frozen, then canned/dry goods. Keep 3 days (ideally 2 weeks) of food that needs no cooking.
  2. Discard food touched by flood water, and anything above 40°F (4°C) for over 2 hours.

🎣 Fishing basics

  1. Simple kit: line, small hooks, sinkers, bobber; bait with worms, insects or bread.
  2. Fish early morning and evening near cover (logs, docks, weed edges).
  3. Most places require fishing licenses for adults (in Florida: FWC); follow size/bag limits. Cook fish thoroughly.

🪤 Trapping (only where legal)

Trapping is tightly regulated in most places (in Florida: FWC). In a true survival emergency, simple snares or fish traps are a last resort; know the law first and never trap protected species.

🌿 Foraging — "if in doubt, don't"

⚠️ Many deadly plants look like edible ones. Only eat plants you can identify with certainty from multiple reliable sources or an expert. The Army "universal edibility test" is slow, risky and not a shortcut — it does not protect you from the most dangerous poisonous plants or mushrooms. Never eat wild mushrooms unless an expert identifies them.

🧂 Preserving

  • Drying: thin strips in sun/wind or low heat until brittle; keep insects off.
  • Salting: pack in plenty of salt to draw out moisture; rinse/soak before cooking.
  • Smoking: slow, low smoke over hardwood for many hours; hot-smoke to a safe internal temperature.
🌴 Hot-humid climate notes: heat and humidity spoil food fast — preserve or eat it quickly. Coconuts, citrus and saw palmetto berries are known foods, but only harvest where legal and with permission. Shellfish can carry red-tide toxins; check FWC/health advisories.

🩹 First aid

🚨 Order of priorities

  1. Scene safe? Don't become a second victim.
  2. Call 911 (or send someone).
  3. Severe bleeding — stop it now.
  4. Airway & breathing — not breathing normally? Start CPR (push hard and fast in the center of the chest).
  5. Shock — keep warm, lying down; then treat other injuries.

🩸 Stop the bleed

  1. Apply firm, direct pressure with a cloth or gauze; don't lift to check.
  2. For deep wounds on arms/legs/junctions, pack the wound tightly with gauze or clean cloth and keep pressing hard.
  3. Life-threatening limb bleeding that pressure won't stop: apply a tourniquet 2–3 inches above the wound (not on a joint), tighten until bleeding stops, note the time. Take a Stop the Bleed class.

🧼 Wound cleaning

  1. Wash hands; rinse the wound with clean (treated) water for several minutes.
  2. Cover with a clean dressing; change daily.
  3. Watch for infection: spreading redness, warmth, swelling, pus, red streaks or fever → seek care.

🔥 Burns

  1. Cool with cool running water for about 20 minutes. No ice, butter or creams.
  2. Cover loosely with clean, non-stick dressing. Large, deep, face/hand/genital or electrical burns → 911/medical care.
🌡 Heat illness (critical in hot climates): Heat exhaustion (heavy sweating, cramps, weakness, nausea): move to shade/AC, loosen clothing, cool wet cloths, sip water. Heat stroke (confusion, very high body temp, may stop sweating, collapse): call 911 and cool the person immediately with water and fanning, ice packs to neck/armpits/groin.

🧰 Basic med kit

  • Gauze pads & rolls, adhesive bandages, medical tape, elastic bandage
  • Tourniquet (commercial, e.g. CAT/SOF-T) and pressure bandage
  • Gloves, trauma shears, tweezers, thermometer
  • Antiseptic wipes, saline/clean water for rinsing
  • Oral rehydration salts, emergency blanket, instant cold packs
  • Over-the-counter pain/fever, antihistamine, anti-diarrheal — use only per label
  • Your prescriptions, list of meds and allergies
💊 Take prescriptions exactly as directed by your doctor/pharmacist and over-the-counter medicines only as the label says. This guide gives no dosing advice.

🧭 Navigation

🗺 Map & compass basics

  1. Carry a paper map of your area (download the PDFs in the Maps tab ahead of time).
  2. Learn the map legend: roads, water, contour lines (land shape), scale.
  3. Hold the compass flat, away from metal and phones.

🧭 Orient the map

  1. Lay the compass edge along a north–south grid line of the map.
  2. Turn map and compass together until the needle points to map north (adjust for declination: it varies by place — check the current NOAA value for your location).
  3. Now features on the map line up with the real world.

🏞 Terrain association

Match what you see (roads, lakes, rivers, power lines, towers, bridges) to the map. Tick off landmarks as you pass them; know your "catching feature" (a road or river that stops you if you overshoot).

☀️ Sun & ⭐ stars

  • The sun rises roughly in the east and sets roughly in the west; around midday it is roughly due south in the Northern Hemisphere (due north in the Southern Hemisphere).
  • At night find the North Star (Polaris): follow the two "pointer" stars at the end of the Big Dipper's cup. Its height above the horizon roughly equals your latitude.

📣 Signaling

  1. 3 of anything = distress: 3 whistle blasts, 3 fires in a triangle, 3 rock piles, 3 flashes.
  2. Whistle: carries far and saves your voice — 3 blasts, pause, repeat.
  3. Signal mirror: aim the reflected flash at aircraft/ships using your fingers as a sight; sweep the horizon even if you see no one.
  4. Smoke: in daylight add green leaves to a fire for white smoke (visible over dark terrain) — only where safe.
  5. Ground signals: large contrasting "SOS" or "X" in open areas.

📻 Radio

  • NOAA Weather Radio: official alerts 24/7; get one with battery/crank power and SAME alerting.
  • FRS walkie-talkies: no license, short range — good for family/neighbors. GMRS: more power and repeaters; needs an FCC license (no exam).
  • Ham (amateur) radio: long range and emergency nets; requires an FCC license exam. Local ARES/RACES groups help in disasters.
  • Meshtastic: cheap LoRa radios that relay short text messages off-grid between nearby users — no license or cell service needed.

🛡 Security & situational awareness

  1. Low profile: don't advertise supplies, generators or fuel. Keep lights and noise modest at night.
  2. OPSEC: don't post your location, stockpiles or travel plans online.
  3. Stay in groups and check on neighbors — community is the best security.
  4. Avoid crowds, protests and unrest. If caught in one, move to the edges and away; know two exits.
  5. Home hardening basics: solid doors with good deadbolts and long strike-plate screws, window locks, motion lights, storm shutters (also helps in hurricanes), a fire extinguisher and smoke/CO alarms.
  6. De-escalation: stay calm, keep distance, speak slowly, don't argue, give people a way out, and leave if you can. Call 911 when there's a threat.

🌿 Plants

⚠️ Read first: Plant ID mistakes can kill. Confirm every identification with multiple reliable sources or a local expert (UF/IFAS Extension, a botanist) before touching or using any plant. This is educational, not medical advice — "traditionally used for" means historical/folk use, not proven treatment. Don't use plants if pregnant, nursing, on medications, or for children without a doctor's advice. Suspected poisoning: call Poison Control 1-800-222-1222 (24/7, free) or 911 if the person is unconscious, having seizures or trouble breathing.

🌱 Beginner medicinal plants (traditionally used for…)

  • 🍃 Broadleaf plantain (Plantago major) — common lawn "weed": low rosette of oval leaves with strong parallel veins (stringy when torn), pencil-like seed spikes. Traditionally used for soothing insect bites, stings and minor skin irritation as a crushed-leaf poultice.
  • 🌼 Yarrow (Achillea millefolium) — feathery, finely divided fern-like leaves; flat-topped clusters of small white flowers. Traditionally used for minor cuts (as a styptic). ⚠️ Look-alikes in the carrot family (e.g. water hemlock) are deadly — never confuse feathery leaves with umbrella-shaped flower clusters on hollow, purple-blotched stems. Can cause skin reactions in people allergic to ragweed/daisies.
  • 🌳 Willow bark (Salix spp.) — long narrow leaves, often near water. Inner bark contains salicin, an aspirin-like compound; traditionally used for pain and fever. ⚠️ Same cautions as aspirin: not for children or teens (Reye's syndrome risk), not for anyone allergic to aspirin, with bleeding disorders, ulcers, or on blood thinners, and not in pregnancy.
  • 🫐 Elderberry (Sambucus nigra ssp. canadensis) — shrub with compound leaves (5–11 toothed leaflets), flat clusters of tiny white flowers, then dark purple-black berries. Traditionally used for colds and flu as a cooked syrup. ⚠️ Only fully ripe berries, and only cooked. Raw berries, unripe berries, leaves, stems, bark and roots contain toxic compounds that cause nausea, vomiting and worse.
  • 🌻 Dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) — toothed leaves in a rosette, single yellow flower per hollow stem with milky sap. Leaves, flowers and roots are edible; traditionally used as a mild diuretic and digestive bitter. Avoid plants from sprayed lawns or roadsides; may interact with some medications.
  • 🪴 Aloe vera — thick, spiky-edged succulent leaves with clear gel. Traditionally used for minor burns and sunburn (gel on skin only). Don't eat it — the yellow latex under the skin is a strong laxative.
  • 💜 American beautyberry (Callicarpa americana) — Florida native shrub with clusters of bright purple berries hugging the stems in fall. Crushed leaves were traditionally used as an insect repellent rubbed on skin (test a small area first). Berries are not a good food raw.

☠️ Toxic plants — know these (examples from the southeastern US)

  • 🍂 Poison ivy — "leaves of three, let it be": three leaflets, the middle one on a longer stalk; vine with hairy rootlets or a shrub. Urushiol oil causes blistering rash. Wash skin with soap and cool water within minutes; wash clothes and tools. Never burn it (smoke harms lungs).
  • 🍁 Atlantic poison oak — low shrub, three leaflets lobed like oak leaves, fuzzy undersides; sandy pinelands. Same urushiol rash as poison ivy.
  • 🌿 Poison sumac — shrub/small tree in swamps and wet areas; 7–13 smooth-edged leaflets on red stems, drooping white/gray berries. Very strong urushiol rash.
  • 🌸 Oleander — common Florida landscape shrub: long narrow leathery leaves, pink/white/red flowers. All parts are highly toxic to people and pets (heart poison) — never use the wood for cooking skewers or burn it.
  • 🌱 Castor bean — large star-shaped (palmate) leaves often reddish, spiny seed pods; mottled bean-like seeds contain ricin. Seeds are deadly if chewed.
  • ☂️ Water hemlock — wet ditches and marshes: umbrella-shaped clusters of small white flowers, hollow stems often purple-streaked, chambered root. One of the most toxic plants in North America — even a small bite can cause fatal seizures. Avoid all carrot-family look-alikes.
  • 🔴 Rosary pea — invasive vine with feathery compound leaves; shiny red seeds with a black spot. Contains abrin; a chewed seed can be fatal. Never use the seeds for jewelry.
  • 🟠 Lantana — common shrub with clusters of small multicolored flowers (yellow/orange/pink); green (unripe) berries are toxic and leaves are toxic to livestock and pets. Don't eat the berries.
  • 🍏 Manchineel — rare, coastal South Florida/Keys tree with small green apple-like fruit; often marked with warning signs. Extremely toxic: the fruit, the milky sap, and even rainwater dripping off it burn skin and eyes. Never shelter under it or burn it.
  • 🌶 Brazilian pepper — invasive shrub/tree everywhere in Central/South Florida: compound leaves with 3–13 leaflets and a peppery smell when crushed, clusters of bright red berries in winter. Related to poison ivy — sap and blooms cause rash and breathing irritation for many people.
  • 🌺 Coral bean (Erythrina herbacea) — native shrub with tall spikes of red tubular flowers and pods with bright red seeds; seeds are poisonous.
  • 🌙 Jimsonweed (Datura) — large trumpet-shaped white/purple flowers, foul-smelling leaves, spiny seed pods. All parts are toxic (hallucinations, high heart rate, death).
📞 Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222 — save it in your phone. Take a photo of the plant (or bring a sample in a bag) to help responders. Learn more from UF/IFAS Extension (EDIS).
